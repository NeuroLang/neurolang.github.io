Ontologies#

Which answers does a query owe when the data are incomplete and an ontology says what must exist beyond them? The classical answer, a rewriting of the query into a union of conjunctive queries, is finite only for restricted ontology languages and can be exponential even there. NeuroLang chases the axioms that create no value as Datalog rules, and rewrites the query with the others by XRewrite [Gottlob2014].

This page states what that procedure computes, why it is split in two, for which ontologies its answers are complete, and what negation, aggregation and probabilities mean over the predicates of an ontology. The approach, and its use on neuroscience ontologies, is described in [Zanitti2023].

Three instances#

Three ontologies are used throughout this page.

(I) Deans and units. Every dean heads a department, and every department is a unit:

@prefix rdfs: <http://www.w3.org/2000/01/rdf-schema#> .
@prefix owl: <http://www.w3.org/2002/07/owl#> .
@prefix : <http://ex.org/> .
:Dean a owl:Class ; rdfs:subClassOf [
    a owl:Restriction ; owl:onProperty :headOf ; owl:someValuesFrom :Dept
] .
:Dept a owl:Class ; rdfs:subClassOf :Unit .
:Unit a owl:Class .

with the facts Dean('Juan'), headOf('Ana', 'Physics') and Dept('Physics'). Nothing names the department Juan heads.

(II) A class closed under a property. A ⊑ ∀p.A, where A is a class and p a property: whatever an A reaches through p is an A. On the facts A(a0) and p(a0, a1), …, p(an-1, an), with a0 to an named values, every ai is an A.

(III) The Cognitive Atlas. The neurosynth and neurostore engines load cogat.v2.owl (see Querying the Cognitive Atlas Ontology). It gives 10,405 constraints: three for each of its 2,724 someValuesFrom restrictions, one for each of its 1,797 subclass axioms, 430 allValuesFrom restrictions and 6 domain and range axioms. The 430 allValuesFrom constraints lie in one recursive component of 3,364 predicates. The engine has no instance of any class. We add one, of the class CAO_01600, to measure what happens when data meets the hierarchy. The restrictions themselves are facts on class names; the 323 someValuesFrom restrictions on ro:has_part are:

ans(c, d) :- rdfs:subClassOf(c, r),
    owl:onProperty(r, <http://www.obofoundry.org/ro/ro.owl#has_part>),
    owl:someValuesFrom(r, d)

Run in the browser REPL

These instances cover what the procedure must handle: a value no fact names in (I), a recursion in (II), and the size of a real ontology in (III). A fourth, (IV), puts (III) to work on the studies of Neurosynth, in Open and closed worlds on the Cognitive Atlas.

Loading an ontology#

load_ontology() reads OWL or RDF files with rdflib and splits them in two. The TBox, the axioms on classes, becomes constraints, body → head implications kept apart from the rules of the program. The ABox, the facts on individuals, becomes relations: the data added with add_tuple_set, or, with individuals=True, every triple s p o as the fact p(s, o), an s rdf:type C making s an instance of C, C(s), as do the types a program states (Types). On (I), with the Turtle above in deans.ttl and loaded from Python as in An ontology:

from neurolang.frontend import NeurolangPDL

nl = NeurolangPDL()
nl.load_ontology("deans.ttl")
nl.add_tuple_set([("Juan",)], name="Dean")
nl.add_tuple_set([("Ana", "Physics")], name="headOf")
nl.add_tuple_set([("Physics",)], name="Dept")

OntologyParser reads the axioms of each class C and each property p as follows, where x, y and z are variables, q a property, D and B classes, v a value and aux a fresh predicate:

OWL axiom

constraints

C rdfs:subClassOf D

C(x) → D(x)

C ⊑ ∃p.D (someValuesFrom)

C(x) → ∃y aux(x, y), aux(x, y) → p(x, y), aux(x, y) → D(y)

C ⊑ ∀p.D (allValuesFrom)

C(x) ∧ p(x, y) → D(y)

C ⊑ ∃p.{v} (hasValue)

C(x) → p(x, v)

C ⊑ B ⊓ ∃p.D (intersectionOf)

C(x) → B(x) and the someValuesFrom constraints

annotation C p v, as rdfs:label

C(x) → p(x, v)

p rdfs:subPropertyOf q

p(x, y) → q(x, y)

p owl:equivalentProperty q

p(x, y) → q(x, y), q(x, y) → p(x, y)

p owl:inverseOf q

p(x, y) → q(y, x), q(y, x) → p(x, y)

p rdfs:domain D

p(x, y) → D(x)

p rdfs:range D

p(x, y) → D(y)

p a owl:SymmetricProperty

p(x, y) → p(y, x)

p a owl:TransitiveProperty

p(x, y) ∧ p(y, z) → p(x, z)

Furthermore, the structure of the TBox is kept as facts on class names, neurolang:subClassOf('C', 'D') and neurolang:label('C', 'v'), so a query can walk the hierarchy itself. unionOf, complementOf, equivalentClass, enumerated classes and cardinality restrictions are read with a warning and dropped, as is a property axiom on a class expression, such as the range A ⊔ B or the anonymous inverse [ owl:inverseOf p ]. A datatype range, as xsd:integer, gives no constraint, and property chains, functional and reflexive properties are not read. Dropping an axiom removes answers, never adds one. Hence the constraints NeuroLang uses are a subset of the ontology’s, and every answer is entailed by the ontology.

Open world and certain answers#

A relation of a Datalog program is closed: what is not derived is false. An ontology is read under the open-world assumption: the facts are a part of the world, and the constraints say what else the world must contain. The answers a query owes are its certain answers, the tuples of named values true in every world that contains the facts and satisfies the constraints. A predicate of the ontology’s signature of which no tuple is entailed, as one only in the bodies of constraints and without facts or rules, has the empty relation as its certain answers.

The chase computes them [Fagin2005]. It applies each constraint whose body holds and whose head does not, and stands an unnamed value, a labelled null, for each ∃y. Its result, the universal model, maps into every world of the ontology. Hence a query’s certain answers are its answers on the universal model with no unnamed value [Cali2013].

On (I), the chase adds aux('Juan', n1), headOf('Juan', n1), Dept(n1) and Unit(n1), with n1 unnamed, and Unit('Physics'). The query

ans(x) :- headOf(x, y), Unit(y)

has the certain answers Ana and Juan; ans(x, y) :- headOf(x, y), Unit(y) has only ('Ana', 'Physics'), as Juan’s department has no name. A constraint C ⊑ ∃p.C makes the chase infinite, so it is not run as such. NeuroLang runs the part of it that ends, and rewrites the query for the rest.

Two mechanisms#

A constraint is full if every variable of its head is in its body; it is existential otherwise. Subclass, allValuesFrom, hasValue, annotation, property and aux(x, y) → p(x, y) constraints are full; C(x) → ∃y aux(x, y) is existential.

The chase of full constraints. A full constraint is a Datalog rule: Dept(x) → Unit(x) is Unit(x) :- Dept(x). Its chase creates no value and ends, and its result is the least model of the rules. NeuroLang adds to the program of a query the full constraints its predicates depend on, among those whose body can hold from the predicates with facts or rules. A predicate with facts that such a rule derives is replaced, in the rules of the query, by a copy holding its facts and what the rules derive, as Datalog does not let a rule derive into a relation of facts.

The rewriting of existential constraints. XRewrite [Gottlob2011] [Gottlob2014] replaces a query atom by the body of a constraint whose head unifies with it. An existential constraint applies only if the variable in its ∃ position is a variable of the query that occurs nowhere else and is not in the head. A factorisation step first unifies atoms that share that variable. On (I), the rewriting of the query is the union of eight rules, among which

ans(x) :- headOf(x, y), Unit(y)
ans(x) :- headOf(x, y), Dept(y)
ans(x) :- aux(x, y), Dept(y)
ans(x) :- aux(x, y), aux(z, y)
ans(x) :- aux(x, y)
ans(x) :- Dean(x)

Unit(y) becomes Dept(y), then aux(z, y) by the full constraint aux(z, y) → Dept(y); the two aux atoms are factorised into aux(x, y), where y occurs once; finally, the existential constraint rewrites aux(x, y) into Dean(x). The rules over aux, which has no values, are dropped before evaluation.

Dead rewritings. Some rewritings are dropped as soon as they appear, with all those they would give. Let P be a predicate without facts or rules whose constraints are all existential in a position i, as aux, and P(…) an atom of a rewriting with the term t in position i. P(…) can only be rewritten once t is a variable that occurs nowhere else. A constant, a variable of the head and an occurrence in a negated atom stay so in every rewriting. A rewriting step on another atom keeps t, at the positions of the constraint’s variable it unifies with, since a full constraint has all its head’s variables in its body and an existential one applies to t in its existential position only if t occurs once. Hence t leaves the other atoms only by becoming the term in position i of an atom over P that is factorised with this one. NeuroLang follows, for each occurrence of t, the predicates and positions the rewriting steps that keep it can move it to; if one occurrence cannot reach position i of P, every rewriting of the query has an atom over P, which has no values, and none is evaluated. On (III) these are the rewritings aux_i(s, c), aux_j(z, c) of measures(s, c), CAO_00587(c) with i ≠ j: c is shared, in the existential position of both, and neither atom can become the other. The rewritings evaluated are the same with and without this step.

The test needs only the atoms a step adds, so it is made before the step builds its query. NeuroLang puts the terms of the rewritten atom in place of the variables of the constraint’s head in its body, gives the body’s other variables names no query has, and tests the query that results. It is the query the step would build, up to the names renaming gives those variables, and the test looks only at which terms are equal, constant or in the head. A step whose query is dead is not built. When the head of the constraint has a constant or a repeated variable, its unifier binds terms of the query: that step is built and tested as before. The rewritings are the same, their variable names included.

The rules of the program that define a predicate in the body of a constraint are solved with the query, even when the query names only what the constraint derives: the code a query needs is followed through the constraints. With Dean(x) :- professor(x), chairs(x) in place of the facts of Dean, ans(x) :- headOf(x, y) is rewritten into ans(x) :- Dean(x), and the rule of Dean is part of the program that answers it.

The split. Full constraints are in the rewriting too: (I) needs Dept ⊑ Unit applied to Juan’s unnamed department, which the chase never creates. However, they rewrite only an atom with a variable out of the rule’s head, the only kind of atom that can stand for an unnamed value. An atom whose variables are all in the head, as Dean(x) above, stands for named values only, on which the chase has already applied every full constraint. The rewritings left are evaluated, with the chased rules, by NeuroLang’s Datalog engine. Hence each full constraint reaches the named values through the chase, and the unnamed ones only through the atoms that can stand for them.

Two kinds of full constraints depart from this rule. Firstly, a constraint whose body is one atom with a variable out of its head, as the domain p(x, y) → D(x), derives a fact on named values from an atom with an unnamed one. On (I), with headOf rdfs:domain Manager, Manager('Juan') holds through Juan’s unnamed department, which the chase never creates. NeuroLang rewrites such a constraint as the rule Manager(x) :- headOf(x, y), whose atom headOf(x, y) has a variable out of the head, and adds its rewritings, among which Manager(x) :- Dean(x), to the chased rules. Hence Manager('Juan') is a named fact of the chase, and every full constraint applies to it. Secondly, transitivity, p(x, y) ∧ p(y, z) → p(x, z), is applied by the chase only. A rewriting step with it replaces p(x, y) by p(x, w) ∧ p(w, y), whose two atoms it rewrites again, without end.

Why split#

A union of conjunctive queries is a first-order query. Hence a finite rewriting exists only if the certain answers are first-order definable, and XRewrite ends only when one exists. Linear and sticky constraints [Cali2012] [Cali2012b], among which the DL-Lite family behind OWL 2 QL [Calvanese2007], have that property. allValuesFrom does not. On (II), A(ai) holds exactly when ai is reachable from an A through p. Reachability is not first-order definable, so no union of conjunctive queries gives the certain answers of ans(x) :- A(x); XRewrite produces A(x), A(z) ∧ p(z, x), A(z) ∧ p(z, w) ∧ p(w, x), … without end. The chase computes it as the transitive closure it is.

Moreover, where the rewriting is finite it can be exponential in the query: each atom is replaced by any of the classes below its own, and the alternatives multiply across atoms [Calvanese2007].

(III) shows both effects. The query ans(x) :- ro:has_part(x, y) is rewritten with the constraints whose body can hold: none without an instance, so it runs in 0.7 s. One instance of CAO_01600 makes 8,566 of the 10,405 constraints hold. Rewriting with all of them, as NeuroLang did before the split, did not end within 120 s. With the split, the full constraints run in the chase, the rewriting holds 333 conjunctive queries, built in 0.8 s, and the query returns the instance in 1.7 s. rdflib gives the same answer: a superclass of CAO_01600 has a someValuesFrom restriction on ro:has_part.

When the answers are complete#

The answers are always sound: a chased rule and a rewriting step each derive only what the ontology entails, and dropping a step only loses answers. They are the certain answers under three assumptions.

Firstly, the constraints are Horn: one atom in each head, no disjunction. With unionOf on the right of a subclass axiom, query answering is coNP-hard in the data [Calvanese2013], and, unless PTime = coNP, no Datalog program computes it. NeuroLang drops these axioms, so its answers are then incomplete.

Secondly, no full constraint with two or more atoms in its body derives an atom over named values only from an atom with an unnamed value. A constraint with one such atom is chased with its rewritings, as above. Under this assumption the named facts of the universal model are those of the chase of full constraints and of these rewritings. A query atom that the rewriting must replace then stands for an atom with an unnamed value, so it has a variable out of the head: the rewritings left out are those that only re-derive named facts, and XRewrite’s completeness argument [Gottlob2014] goes through with the remaining steps. An intersectionOf, once read as aux(x, y) → B(x), is now read as C(x) → B(x). The constraints of the parser satisfy the assumption, with two exceptions. The first is an allValuesFrom on a property that leads from an unnamed value to a named one: a property to which a hasValue restriction or an annotation gives a constant, the inverse of a property in a someValuesFrom, or a symmetric one. There, an unnamed x with C(x) and p(x, v) makes D(v) hold for the named v, and NeuroLang does not find it. On (I), with headedBy the inverse of headOf and Dept ⊑ ∀headedBy.Boss, Juan is a Boss, and is not returned. The second is transitivity, which the rewriting never applies. With a symmetric and transitive headOf, headOf('Juan', n1) and headOf(n1, 'Juan') give headOf('Juan', 'Juan'), which NeuroLang does not find. Moreover, a chain through unnamed values is lost too: with A ⊑ ∃p.B, B ⊑ ∃p.C and a transitive p, ans(x) :- p(x, y), C(y) misses the instances of A. In general, a transitive property makes a certain answer a question of reachability, which no union of conjunctive queries expresses, as (II) shows; OWL 2 QL, the profile designed for such rewritings, has no transitive properties.

Finally, the rewriting ends. It does on (I) and on (III), with 333 rewritings for ro:has_part and 615 for ro:part_of. The rewritings of the domain and range constraints, computed once for the predicates with values, are part of the program of every query: if one of them does not end, no query does. On random ontologies of 4 classes whose full constraints are not recursive, the answers of 1,080 random queries of 1 to 3 atoms match those of a chase with unnamed values bounded at depth 6; 120 of them are the test test_certain_answers_on_random_horn_ontologies. With four checks running at once, three of the 1,080 took between 5 and 11 s, and one more than 5 minutes, alone: its two atoms over variables out of the head, C3(y) and C2(x), are rewritten independently and their alternatives multiply. The rewriting need not end at all when recursive allValuesFrom constraints apply to a variable out of the head, as C1 ⊑ ∀p.C2, C2 ⊑ C1 and C1 ⊑ ∃p.C3 with the query ans(y) :- C3(z), p(z, y).

The same multiplication sets the time on (III). With one instance of the contrast cogat:CAO_01210 (N-back 2-back, which measures some working memory), ans(s) :- cogat:measures(s, c) takes 7 s, and ans(s) :- cogat:measures(s, c), cogat:CAO_00587(c) 60 s without the dropping of dead rewritings: the 522 someValuesFrom restrictions on measures give as many aux_i(s, c) for the first atom, the subclasses of working memory and the restrictions to them as many atoms for the second, and XRewrite explores their 26,600 pairs. Dropping the dead ones leaves 224 to explore; testing each step for them before building it, as above, the query takes 2.0 s, and ans(s) :- cogat:measures(s, c) 6.5 s. A query of three such atoms, ans(s) :- cogpo:has_contrast(s, x), cogat:measures(x, c), cogat:CAO_00487(c) (the tasks with a contrast that measures some response inhibition), did not end within 16 minutes when its dead rewritings were built and then dropped, 146,000 of them in its first 210 s; with the tests, (IV) asks it of the Stroop studies in 31.0 s.

Outside these assumptions the answers stay sound. On 480 more random queries, of up to 4 atoms, over ontologies with hasValue and, for half of them, recursive full constraints, none returned an answer that is not certain. One missed an answer, through the hasValue exception above, and 7, all over recursive constraints, did not end within 10 s.

The property axioms were checked the same way. On 720 more random queries, of 1 to 3 atoms, over the ontologies of the first check with 1 to 3 property axioms added, drawn from all seven kinds for half of the queries and without transitivity for the others, the bounded chase was run at depths 3 and 5, and the two agreed. For 15 queries it did not end within 30 s; of the 705 others, 699 got the certain answers and none an answer that is not certain. Three missed an answer, all through an allValuesFrom reached back from an unnamed value by a symmetric or inverse property. Three did not end within 10 s, all with an allValuesFrom on a property that an inverse or a domain and a sub-property axiom also constrain. The test test_certain_answers_with_property_axioms runs 48 more.

One more limit concerns rules. XRewrite rewrites each rule of the program on its own, so an unnamed value does not pass from one rule to another: with heads(x, y) :- headOf(x, y), ans(x) :- heads(x, y) returns Ana and not Juan, as y is in the head of heads. heads(x) :- headOf(x, y) returns both. A class defined by a rule over a probabilistic predicate, as Dean(x) :- p(x) with p a probabilistic fact, gives no instance to the constraints of a deterministic query. These assumptions delimit the guarantee: complete answers for Horn ontologies in which allValuesFrom reaches no named value from an unnamed one and no existential value meets a transitive property, for queries whose rewriting ends.

Negation and aggregation#

Negation and aggregation are evaluated on the relations NeuroLang derives: the chase of the full constraints and the rewritten rules. They are closed world over those derived facts, not over the worlds of the ontology. On (I), with a relation person holding Juan, Ana and Eva,

leads(x) :- headOf(x, y), Unit(y)
ans(x) :- person(x), ~leads(x)

returns Eva: she is not known to lead a unit, which does not mean the ontology excludes it. ~Unit(y) likewise holds of every value not derived to be a unit.

An aggregate counts the named values of its argument, over the union of the rewritings of its body. ans(count(x)) :- headOf(x, y) gives 2, Ana and Juan. ans(x, count(y)) :- headOf(x, y) gives ('Ana', 1) and no row for Juan, whose department is unnamed: a count over an ontology predicate is a lower bound on the count in any world of the ontology.

Ontologies and probabilities#

The constraints apply to the deterministic rules of a program, solved before its probabilistic ones. A probabilistic rule reads an ontology predicate through a deterministic rule that queries it. On (I), with the probabilistic fact funded holding Juan with probability 0.5 and Ana with 0.2,

leads(x) :- headOf(x, y), Unit(y)
ans(x, PROB(x)) :- funded(x), leads(x)

gives Juan 0.5 and Ana 0.2. Written directly, ans(x, PROB(x)) :- funded(x), headOf(x, y) gives only Ana, because the body of a probabilistic rule is not rewritten. The ontology thus acts as certain knowledge: it defines deterministic relations, and the probabilistic layer of [Zanitti2023] reasons on top of them.

Open and closed worlds on the Cognitive Atlas#

Which studies used a task that, according to the Cognitive Atlas, measures response inhibition? And which form of response inhibition did each one measure?

The data record particular facts, while an ontology records general ones. The curators of the Cognitive Atlas state each general fact once and leave the particulars out. Read in an open world, these general facts answer questions about particular studies; read in a closed world, they answer none.

Two readings. In the closed world, what is not recorded is false. In the open world, what is not recorded is unknown, and the ontology can imply it. The Atlas says that every Stroop task has an incongruent − congruent contrast, that this contrast measures some response inhibition, and that motor inhibition is a kind of response inhibition. It names no study, no contrast of a particular study and no particular instance of a concept; nor does it decide which form of response inhibition the contrast measures. Under the closed world, the curators would have to enter every study, its contrasts and what each measures before a query found anything. Under the open world, what they left out stays unknown, and the general facts still yield answers.

NeuroLang reads the predicates an ontology constrains in the open world. It returns their certain answers, those true in every completion of the data that agrees with the ontology (Open world and certain answers). Each axiom is a constraint, an implication body → head kept apart from the rules of the program (Loading an ontology). A constraint is existential when its head states that some value exists without naming it; such a value is an unnamed value. The constraints are applied in two ways (Two mechanisms): the chase adds, as rules would, the facts that the constraints without existentials imply, and the rewriting turns the query into a union of queries over the data that also finds the answers holding through unnamed values.

(IV) Experiments and studies in the Cognitive Atlas. The neurosynth engine holds 14,371 studies and loads the Atlas, cogat.v2.owl, as in (III). The Atlas has no instance of any task: it is about tasks, contrasts and concepts, not about experiments. Our data say which task each experiment used, by its name, and rdf:type links them: in RDF, which OWL builds on, x rdf:type C means that x is an instance of the class C, and NeuroLang reads it so, for the types a rule states as for those of a file (Types).

% Our data: experiments we ran, and the task of each, by its name.
our_experiment('e1', 'stroop task')
our_experiment('e2', 'stop signal task')
our_experiment('e3', 'go-no-go task')

% Linked to the Cognitive Atlas: each is an instance of the task of that name.
rdf:type(e, t) :- our_experiment(e, n), cogat_term(t, n)

% Does each experiment have a contrast?
ans(e) :- exists(x; cogpo:has_contrast(e, x))

% ... one that measures response inhibition?
ans(e) :- exists(x, c, k; cogpo:has_contrast(e, x), cogat:measures(x, c),
    rdf:type(c, k), cogat_term(k, 'response inhibition'))

% Which contrast does each have?
ans(e, x) :- cogpo:has_contrast(e, x)

No class is named by its identifier: the tasks and the concept are found by their labels, through cogat_term. The questions rest on axioms of the Atlas such as these, where A ⊑ B reads “every A is a B” and ∃p.D “related by the property p to some D”:

Axioms of the Atlas behind (IV)#

In plain English

Formally

Every Stroop task has an incongruent − congruent contrast.

cogat:CAO_00872 ⊑ ∃cogpo:has_contrast.cogat:CAO_01515

Every incongruent − congruent contrast of the Stroop task measures some response inhibition.

cogat:CAO_01515 ⊑ ∃cogat:measures.cogat:CAO_00487

Motor inhibition is a kind of response inhibition.

cogat:CAO_00407 ⊑ cogat:CAO_00487

cogat:CAO_00872 is the Stroop task, cogat:CAO_01515 its contrast Stroop_task-incongruent_minus_congruent, cogat:CAO_00487 response inhibition and cogat:CAO_00407 motor inhibition; cogat: is the namespace http://www.cognitiveatlas.org/ontology/cogat.owl#, and cogpo: that of the Cognitive Paradigm Ontology, from which the Atlas takes has_contrast. The go/no-go task (cogat:CAO_00827) has a contrast that measures some response inhibition too. The stop signal task (cogat:CAO_00871) has six contrasts, and none has a measures axiom. Every identifier, axiom and count on the Atlas in this section was checked with rdflib on the engine’s copy of the file.

The closed world is computed on the same engine with the constraints removed and cogpo:has_contrast and cogat:measures as empty relations: the plain Datalog reading of the same program. The results were computed on 15 November 2026 with NeuroLang master 167e48b0 and the reading of rdf:type this section describes, one query at a time, each stopped after 1,500 s; each time includes the compilation of the query.

The curators stated one axiom; they did not enter 139 contrasts. The same 139 studies mention the Stroop task, mentions(s, 'stroop task'), in 1.4 s. The question, however, is about a concept, and the ontology decides which tasks count: a curator who gives another task a contrast that measures response inhibition changes the answer without changing the query. Of the tasks of the Atlas with a contrast that measures response inhibition, only 'stroop task' is a term of Neurosynth; the vocabulary of the neurostore engine has five of them.

The Atlas, twice. Once loaded, the Atlas is in the engine in two forms. Its axioms are constraints over one predicate per class and per property (Loading an ontology): the open world above. Its triples are also facts on the names of its classes: rdfs:subClassOf, owl:onProperty, owl:someValuesFrom and rdfs:label relate a class to its restrictions and to its label, and the engine’s cogat_measures, cogat_subclass_of and cogat_term join them. Read as facts, the axioms say which tasks have a contrast that measures response inhibition, or one of its subclasses:

has_contrast_class(t, k) :- rdfs:subClassOf(t, r), owl:onProperty(r, <http://www.cogpo.org/ontologies/working/CogPOver2011.owl#has_contrast>), owl:someValuesFrom(r, k)
inhibition(c) :- cogat_term(c, 'response inhibition')
inhibition(c) :- cogat_subclass_of(c, d), cogat_term(d, 'response inhibition')
inhibition_task(t, c) :- has_contrast_class(t, k), cogat_measures(k, c), inhibition(c)
ans(t, n, m) :- inhibition_task(t, c), cogat_term(t, n), cogat_term(c, m)

It returns 7 tasks in 4.1 s: the colour-word Stroop, the conditional stop signal, the choice reaction time, the go/no-go, the Simon and the Stroop tasks, and the Hayling sentence completion test, each with response inhibition. With the same first four rules, ans(s, n) :- inhibition_task(t, c), cogat_term(t, n), mentions(s, n) returns the 139 studies in 4.5 s. This reading needs no instance, but it finds only what the axioms say of the classes as written: it reaches a study through the name of its task, and never through a contrast of the study itself.

Where in the brain? The open-world condition of a probabilistic query:

rdf:type(s, t) :- mentions(s, 'stroop task'), cogat_term(t, 'stroop task')
inhibition_study(s) :- exists(x, c, k; cogpo:has_contrast(s, x),
    cogat:measures(x, c), rdf:type(c, k), cogat_term(k, 'response inhibition'))
p_active_given_inhibition(i, j, k, PROB) :- (activated(i, j, k, s) & selected_study(s)) // (inhibition_study(s) & selected_study(s))
ans(i, j, k, p) :- p_active_given_inhibition(i, j, k, p)

Here p is the probability that a study, among those that measure response inhibition, reports a peak within 6 mm of the voxel (i, j, k). In the closed world the condition holds of no study, and the query returns none, in 2.2 s. What the curators did not state. The studies that mention the stop signal, linked to its task, rdf:type(s, t) :- mentions(s, 'stop signal'), cogat_term(t, 'stop signal task'): 99 of them mention it, in 1.5 s, and none is an inhibition_study(s), in 3.0 s. Types#

rdf:type(x, C) makes x an instance of C, whether a file states it, ex:our_experiment a cogat:CAO_00872, or a rule of the program, rdf:type(e, t) :- our_experiment(e, n), cogat_term(t, n). The facts of rdf:type the files state are kept in a relation of their own, which a rule of rdf:type reads, so that the rules of a program can add types. At each query, NeuroLang finds the classes of which rdf:type gives an instance and makes their instances, C(x), from the data that type them; the other classes keep no instance, and the rewriting drops the rewritings over them (When the answers are complete). The types of a program must come from its data: a rule of rdf:type that depends on a predicate the ontology constrains is refused, as its types would depend on the instances they make.

In a question, rdf:type(c, k), with k fixed by atoms without c and with no other variable, as cogat_term(k, 'response inhibition'), is the atom of the class they name, cogat:CAO_00487(c), which the constraints apply to an unnamed c too. NeuroLang finds the class before rewriting, in the query’s rule and in the rules of the program; a label of several classes gives a rule for each, and a label of none gives no answer. rdf:type(e, t) with t free reads the types stated, those of the files and of the rules; with k fixed, the instances of the class include those of its subclasses.

Writing such queries#

Keep a chain through unnamed values inside one rule. The rewriting treats each rule on its own (When the answers are complete), so inhibition_study(s) holds the whole chain and projects it onto s; a rule with x or c in its head loses the answers reached through the unnamed contrast. Type the instances with a deterministic rule, as with mentions above, and bring PROB in afterwards, as the brain map does.

In short, the open world lets the curators of an ontology be non-exhaustive. On (IV), one axiom stated once gave our experiments and 139 studies a contrast that no one entered, and only what was never stated stayed out of reach.

What would confirm and what limits this#

The completeness claim rests on an argument and on 2,265 random queries, not on a mechanised proof. The W3C OWL test cases [OWLTest2004] check the reading of OWL itself: of the 84 tests of the approved and proposed archives, the 8 entailment and non-entailment tests whose premises are in the fragment NeuroLang reads and whose conclusion is a set of facts on individuals (on allValuesFrom, someValuesFrom, inverseOf, SymmetricProperty, TransitiveProperty and an unread owl:imports) pass, as test_w3c_owl_tests.py. The 76 others are skipped, each with its reason in frontend/tests/w3c_owl_tests/skipped.tsv: 13 consistency and inconsistency tests (NeuroLang does not check consistency), 7 on owl:imports, 5 not about an OWL feature, 44 whose premises use what NeuroLang does not read (functional and inverse functional properties, owl:samePropertyAs, cardinalities, equality of individuals and classes, unionOf, complementOf, oneOf, disjointWith, intersectionOf other than as a superclass, a class expression as a subclass) and 7 whose conclusion is an axiom on classes or properties rather than a query. The OWL 2 test repository no longer resolves. A cross-check against an OWL reasoner, such as HermiT or Konclude, on ontologies with instances of the Cognitive Atlas, would test it at scale. The termination of the rewriting on recursive allValuesFrom is not guaranteed. A Datalog rewriting of the anonymous part, as in the combined approach [Lutz2009] [Kontchakov2011], which materialises one unnamed value per existential constraint and filters the spurious answers, would remove that limit, at the cost of a filtration step for each query. Until then, the guarantee holds for Horn ontologies in which allValuesFrom reaches no named value from an unnamed one and no existential value meets a transitive property, on queries whose rewriting ends.
