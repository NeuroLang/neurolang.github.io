Datalog: A Rule-Based Query Language for NeuroLang#

Besides the SQUALL controlled English, NeuroLang reads Datalog: rules written with :- (implication) instead of English sentences, closer to the logical core of the system.

Every example of this tutorial runs in the REPL (Using the REPL), in the browser or in neurolang-query -i --engine neurosynth: type it at the prompt, or follow Run in the browser REPL under it. An example that uses the rules of earlier ones has no such link: type the earlier ones first.

This tutorial mirrors the Bayes Factor decoding example from the SQUALL: Controlled English for NeuroLang tutorial, showing the same problem expressed in text Datalog. Read the two side by side to see how SQUALL sentences map to Datalog rules.

The full program we will build, on the studies of Neurosynth and the Destrieux atlas (it takes about 20 s):

activates(s, r) :- destrieux_activated(r, s)
region(r) :- destrieux(r, x)

active_region(r) :- region(r), selected_study(s), activates(s, r)
region_probability(r, p_r) :- PROB[active_region(r)] = p_r

mentioned_term(t) :- term(t), selected_study(s), mentions(s, t)
term_probability(t, p_t) :- PROB[mentioned_term(t)] = p_t

cooccurrence(r, t) :-
    region(r), term(t), selected_study(s),
    activates(s, r), mentions(s, t)
joint_probability(r, t, p_rt) :- PROB[cooccurrence(r, t)] = p_rt

bayes_factor(r, t, bf) :-
    joint_probability(r, t, p_rt),
    region_probability(r, p_r),
    term_probability(t, p_t),
    (bf == (p_rt / p_r) / ((p_t - p_rt) / (1.0 - p_r)))

ans(r, t, bf) :- bayes_factor(r, t, bf), (r == 'R G oc temp lat fusifor')

Run in the browser REPL

Part 1: The Bayes Factor Problem#

1.1 What We Are Computing#

Given a set of brain-imaging studies, each reporting activation peaks in some anatomical region and associated with cognitive terms, we want to find which terms are specifically associated with the right fusiform gyrus.

The Bayes Factor quantifies the evidence:

\[\mathrm{BF}(r, t) = \frac{P(T{=}t \mid R{=}r)}{P(T{=}t \mid R{\neq}r)} = \frac{P(R,T)/P(R)}{(P(T) - P(R,T))/(1 - P(R))}\]

where \(P(R,T)\) is the joint probability that a randomly chosen study activates region \(R\) and mentions term \(T\), \(P(R)\) is the marginal probability of activating \(R\), and \(P(T)\) of mentioning \(T\).

We will compute all three probability distributions as Datalog rules, then combine them with arithmetic to produce \(\mathrm{BF}\) — all inside NeuroLang with zero post-hoc pandas computation.

1.2 The Data#

The program uses four relations of the neurosynth engine (.predicates lists them all, with their columns):

study(study_id)

The 14 371 studies of Neurosynth, by PubMed id.

mentions(study_id, term)

The terms each study mentions (TF-IDF above 0.03).

term(term)

The terms of Neurosynth’s vocabulary.

selected_study(study_id)

A uniform probabilistic choice over the studies (see Part 5).

and two it defines from the Destrieux atlas, the anatomical regions and the studies whose peaks fall in them:

activates(s, r) :- destrieux_activated(r, s)
region(r) :- destrieux(r, x)

Run in the browser REPL

The REPL answers defined activates/2 and defined region/1: the rules stay in the session, and the examples below use them. The region we look at is 'R G oc temp lat fusifor', the right fusiform gyrus in the labels of the atlas, which 2 836 of the studies activate.

Part 2: Datalog Preliminaries#

2.1 Predicates as Relations#

A predicate in Datalog names a relation — it is the equivalent of a table name in SQL or a noun in SQUALL. A predicate is written as an identifier followed by parenthesised arguments:

region(x)
study(s)
activates(s, r)

region(x) refers to the region EDB (extensional database) relation. Arguments are variables — they bind to columns of the relation.

2.2 Rules and the :- Operator#

A Datalog rule has the form:

head(x, y, ...) :- body1(x, z), body2(z, y), ...

which reads “head holds if body1, body2, … all hold”. The :- operator is logical implication — it is Datalog’s only means of defining new predicates.

Variables that appear in the head must also appear in the body (the range-restriction property). Variables used only in the body are existentially quantified.

A body may be a disjunction, written | (or ∨); , binds tighter than |, and parentheses group:

uncinate(x, h) :-
    (traverses(x, 'orbitofrontal_gyrus', h)
     | traverses(x, 'middle_frontal_gyrus', h)
     | traverses(x, 'inferior_frontal_gyrus', h)),
    endpoint_in(x, e, 'temporal', h).

stands for three rules, one per disjunct. Each disjunct must bind the variables of the head, and those of its negated atoms. a(x), b(x) | c(x) is (a(x), b(x)) | c(x). Inside parentheses a comparison is a formula of its own, so that values are alternatives of a variable:

ans(i, t) :- cogat_term(i, t), (t == 'motor execution' | t == 'motor planning')

and b(x), (a(x) | x < 3) is b(x), (a(x) | (x < 3)); outside parentheses a comparison keeps its own: b(x), (x < 3).

Note

| is a disjunction everywhere, also inside PROB[...] and MARG[...]: PROB[a(x) | b(x)] = p is the probability of a(x) | b(x). A conditional probability is written with // only. Before, | was a synonym of //.

2.3 The Query Predicate — ans(...)#

The special predicate ans(...) marks a query. The REPL runs it as soon as it is entered and shows its answer as a table:

ans(x) :- region(x)

A rule without ans only defines a relation, for the queries to come.

A submission can hold several queries, each with a name in brackets, ans[name](...); the REPL shows each answer under its name, and .view shows them all at once, as one image per query, with the networks and streamlines of the others:

ans[left](x, y, z) :- activation(x, y, z, 'left hemisphere')
ans[tract](s) :- uncinate(s, 'left')
ans[network](r1, r2, p) :- coactivation(r1, r2, p)

The queries of a submission have each a name: ans stands for the one without brackets, and two queries cannot share a name. They are solved together, which is faster than one after the other when they share predicates; see Several queries at once. In Python, a program’s queries are returned by execute_datalog_program (NeuroLang in Python).

2.4 Variables#

Variables are written as identifiers (letters, digits, underscores). Unlike Prolog, NeuroLang’s text Datalog does not distinguish variables from constants by case — the same identifier can be used in predicate position (functor) or argument position (variable) and context determines its role:

region(r)        % r is a variable in argument position
term(t)          % t is a variable in argument position
Region(R)        % upper-case identifiers work the same way
bigger(x, y) :- size(x, a), size(y, b), (a > b).

Note that in bigger, x and y are bound by the size atoms: a comparison alone cannot bind a head variable (range restriction, §2.2).

For clarity, convention uses short lowercase names for variables that correspond to columns (r for region, t for term, s for study).

To ignore a column, use a variable name that appears only once (the engine creates a fresh anonymous variable for each unique name):

ans(r) :- activates(_study, r).

Here _study appears only once and is treated as a wildcard.

2.5 Constants and Strings#

Constants are written inline:

ans(x) :- region(x), (x == 'R G oc temp lat fusifor').

String literals use single quotes '...'. Numeric constants are written directly: 42, 3.14. The == operator tests equality and works for both strings and numbers (see Part 8 for more comparisons). Comparisons in a rule body must be enclosed in parentheses.

2.6 Comments and Documentation#

A % starts a comment, to the end of the line, unless it is in a string or an IRI. A block of lines starting with %% right before a rule or a fact documents its predicate. Its first line may give the signature of the predicate, the names of its arguments and, after a :, their sorts; the other lines are its description:

%% uncinate(x: streamline, h: hemisphere)
%% The uncinate fasciculus: streamlines joining the orbitofrontal
%% cortex to the anterior temporal lobe through the temporal stem,
%% in hemisphere h.
uncinate(x, h) :- ...

Without a signature, the whole block is the description. The engine keeps the documentation (see NeuroLang in Python for it in Python): the query assistant and the REPL’s .predicates show it, and a column of the sort streamline is drawn as streamlines by .view. The rules are:

  • The block must be right before the statement: a block followed by a blank line, a % comment or nothing is a comment, as a header of a file. A %% after the start of a line, or inside a rule, is a comment too.

  • It documents the predicate of the rule, fact, probabilistic fact or choice, or name := ... statement after it, and the following rules of that predicate need none; it documents nothing before a query or a command.

  • The signature is the first line only if it reads name(...) with the name of that predicate, and then it must give its number of arguments. Each argument is a column: the three columns of a voxel are written i: voxel, j: voxel, k: voxel.

  • The blocks of a predicate add up: their descriptions follow one another, each once, and the arguments of the last signature are kept.

  • The documentation follows the scopes of the program, as its rules do: that of a rule defined in with nl.scope:, or in the REPL’s session that .clear empties, goes with it. A module imported with .import documents its predicates as the program does.

  • The documentation of a predicate in the program wins over its description in the engine’s configuration.

.document(name, "description") in a program documents a predicate too.

Part 3: Predicates and Simple Rules#

3.1 The Bayes Factor Relations#

The Bayes Factor problem needs two binary relations, three unary ones and one probabilistic choice, of the engine or defined in 1.2:

study(s)                % s is a study identifier
region(r)               % r is an anatomical region name
term(t)                 % t is a cognitive term name
activates(s, r)         % study s activates region r
mentions(s, t)          % study s mentions term t
selected_study(s)       % s is a probabilistically chosen study

selected_study is a probabilistic predicate — it is not a regular relation but a uniform probabilistic choice over studies (see Part 5).

3.2 Simple Unary Rules#

The first step in the Bayes Factor program defines intermediate unary predicates that flatten the existential study quantifier:

active_region(r) :- region(r), selected_study(s), activates(s, r).

This reads: r is an active region if there exists a study s such that s is selected and s activates r. The variable s appears only in the body — it is existentially quantified away.

In Datalog notation:

\[\mathtt{active\_region}(r) \leftarrow \mathtt{region}(r) \land \mathtt{selected\_study}(s) \land \mathtt{activates}(s, r)\]

Similarly for terms:

mentioned_term(t) :- term(t), selected_study(s), mentions(s, t).

Part 4: Multi-Variable Rules#

4.1 Compound Rules with Multiple Variables#

To compute the joint distribution we need a binary relation — cooccurrence(region, term) — that links each study’s region and term:

cooccurrence(r, t) :-
    region(r), term(t), selected_study(s),
    activates(s, r), mentions(s, t).

This creates:

\[\mathtt{cooccurrence}(r, t) \leftarrow \mathtt{region}(r) \land \mathtt{term}(t) \land \mathtt{selected\_study}(s) \land \mathtt{activates}(s, r) \land \mathtt{mentions}(s, t).\]

The head variables r and t are bound by the first two body predicates. s appears only in the body and is existentially quantified.

4.2 Conjunction in the Body#

Multiple conditions in a rule body are joined with commas , (or the & operator, which is a synonym):

cooccurrence(r, t) :-
    region(r) & term(t) & selected_study(s) &
    activates(s, r) & mentions(s, t).

Both forms are equivalent. The comma form is more common in Datalog literature.

In the Bayes Factor program, the three intermediate deterministic rules are passed as a single program string:

active_region(r) :- region(r), selected_study(s), activates(s, r).
mentioned_term(t) :- term(t), selected_study(s), mentions(s, t).
cooccurrence(r, t) :-
    region(r), term(t), selected_study(s),
    activates(s, r), mentions(s, t).

Part 5: Probabilistic Queries#

Now we introduce probability — the core of the Bayes Factor computation.

5.1 The Probabilistic Choice#

Probabilistic queries need a predicate that is a probabilistic choice over the studies. The neurosynth engine has one, selected_study: each of its \(N\) studies is the selected one with probability \(1/N\), and exactly one is selected in each possible world. selected_study(s) can appear in rule bodies, and PROB and MARG compute probabilities over it. A choice of your own can be written in Datalog (10.6); the engine’s comes from its configuration (Engine Configuration (engines.yaml)).

5.2 PROB — Marginal Probability#

The PROB[pred(x, ...)] = p body predicate computes the marginal probability of a predicate after marginalising over all unbound variables:

region_probability(r, p_r) :- PROB[active_region(r)] = p_r.

This binds p_r to \(P(\mathtt{active\_region}(r))\) — the fraction of selected studies that activate region r.

The equivalent in the Bayes Factor program:

region_probability(r, p_r) :- PROB[active_region(r)] = p_r.
term_probability(t, p_t) :- PROB[mentioned_term(t)] = p_t.
joint_probability(r, t, p_rt) :- PROB[cooccurrence(r, t)] = p_rt.

A conditioning filter can be added with // (| is a disjunction, not a conditioning, see 2.2):

ans(r, p) :- PROB[active_region(r) // mentioned_term('face')] = p.

This computes the conditional probability \(P(\mathtt{active\_region}(r) \mid \mathtt{mentioned\_term}(\mathit{face}))\), i.e. the fraction of the studies mentioning face that activate r. Either side of // may be a conjunction, in parentheses or not: PROB[(a(x, s), (x < y)) // (b(s), c(s))] = p is PROB[a(x, s), (x < y) // b(s), c(s)] = p.

5.3 MARG — Marginal Probability of a Conjunction#

MARG[...] = p computes the same probability as PROB; it is mostly used with a conjunction inside the brackets:

ans(r, p) :- MARG[selected_study(s), activates(s, r)] = p.

This computes \(P(\exists s.\ \mathtt{selected\_study}(s) \land \mathtt{activates}(s, r))\) for each region r. As with PROB, adding // gives a conditional probability:

ans(r, p) :- MARG[active_region(r) // mentioned_term('face')] = p.

5.4 SUCC — Success Probability#

The parser accepts SUCC[...] = p (and SUCC[...] :- body) for compatibility, but these atoms are currently discarded before execution. Use PROB instead.

Part 6: Connectives#

6.1 Conjunction#

Multiple body atoms are joined with , or &:

p(x, y) :- a(x), b(x, y).
p(x, y) :- a(x) & b(x, y).

Both are equivalent. The comma is the standard Datalog convention.

6.2 Disjunction#

Datalog expresses disjunction through multiple rules with the same head:

p(x) :- a(x).
p(x) :- b(x).

p holds if either a holds or b holds (or both).

6.3 Negation#

Negation-as-failure uses the ~ prefix (the grammar also lists the Unicode ¬, but ~ is the recommended form):

silent_region(r) :- region(r), ~activates(s, r).

This reads: r is a silent region if r is a region and there is no proof that a study activates it — standard negation-as-failure semantics. Every variable of a negated atom must also appear in a positive atom of the rule, or, as s here, appear nowhere else: it is then existentially quantified inside the negation.

Part 7: The Bayes Factor Formula#

7.1 Arithmetic Expressions#

Datalog rules support arithmetic expressions with +, -, *, /, and ** (exponentiation), with standard operator precedence:

(bf == (p_rt / p_r) / ((p_t - p_rt) / (1.0 - p_r)))

Parentheses control grouping, and the whole comparison is itself enclosed in parentheses. The == operator binds the result of the expression to the variable on the left.

7.2 The Complete Bayes Factor Rule#

The BF formula combines the three probability distributions with arithmetic:

bayes_factor(r, t, bf) :-
    joint_probability(r, t, p_rt),
    region_probability(r, p_r),
    term_probability(t, p_t),
    (bf == (p_rt / p_r) / ((p_t - p_rt) / (1.0 - p_r))).

Each probability atom provides a probability column (p_rt, p_r, p_t) that was created by the PROB construct in Part 5. The last line evaluates the Bayes Factor formula inline using arithmetic.

Part 8: Querying and Optimization#

8.1 The ans Query Predicate#

The ans predicate marks a query, run as soon as it is entered; with the rules of Parts 1–7 in the session:

ans(r, t, bf) :-
    bayes_factor(r, t, bf),
    (r == 'R G oc temp lat fusifor')
.sort bf:desc

.sort bf:desc shows the answer with the largest Bayes factors first; in the browser, a click on the column’s header does it.

8.2 Filtering with Comparisons#

The (r == 'R G oc temp lat fusifor') filter uses the equality comparison operator. Text Datalog supports the full comparison suite (always written between parentheses in a rule body):

Operator

Meaning

==

equal

!=

not equal

<

less than

<=

less or equal

>

greater than

>=

greater or equal

Comparisons work on both numeric and string arguments.

8.3 Magic-Sets Optimization#

The (r == 'R G oc temp lat fusifor') filter does more than just post-process — NeuroLang’s magic-sets optimisation pushes the constant backwards through all the rules in the chain:

  1. The constant 'R G oc temp lat fusifor' is inlined into the bayes_factor predicate before the SIPS (Sideways Information Passing Strategy) pass.

  2. The SIPS creates an adorned predicate with the first argument marked as bound.

  3. Magic rules propagate the bound value down the dependency chain: magic_joint_probability(r), magic_region_probability(r), magic_cooccurrence(r), magic_active_region(r).

  4. Each adorned rule filters its body with a magic predicate, so only rows where r = 'R G oc temp lat fusifor' are evaluated.

The result: of the 14 371 studies, only the 2 836 that activate the right fusiform gyrus are evaluated for the joint probabilities, and the query completes in about 20 seconds.

Note

Magic-sets works when the constant appears on an IDB (rule-defined) predicate, as it does here (bayes_factor is defined by a rule). A constant on an EDB predicate does not restrict that predicate, but its rows bind its variables in the IDB predicates right of it: in ans(x) :- e("c", y) & p(y, x), with e an EDB predicate and p defined by rules, the values of y in the rows of e with "c" are passed to p.

Part 9: The Complete Program#

Putting it all together, the program at the top of the page:

% The regions and the studies activating them, from the Destrieux atlas
activates(s, r) :- destrieux_activated(r, s)
region(r) :- destrieux(r, x)

% Deterministic intermediate rules
active_region(r) :- region(r), selected_study(s), activates(s, r)
mentioned_term(t) :- term(t), selected_study(s), mentions(s, t)
cooccurrence(r, t) :-
    region(r), term(t), selected_study(s),
    activates(s, r), mentions(s, t)

% Probabilistic rules — marginal probabilities
region_probability(r, p_r) :- PROB[active_region(r)] = p_r
term_probability(t, p_t) :- PROB[mentioned_term(t)] = p_t
joint_probability(r, t, p_rt) :- PROB[cooccurrence(r, t)] = p_rt

% Bayes Factor formula
bayes_factor(r, t, bf) :-
    joint_probability(r, t, p_rt),
    region_probability(r, p_r),
    term_probability(t, p_t),
    (bf == (p_rt / p_r) / ((p_t - p_rt) / (1.0 - p_r)))

% Query — filter for the target region
ans(r, t, bf) :- bayes_factor(r, t, bf), (r == 'R G oc temp lat fusifor')
.sort bf:desc

Part 10: Advanced Features#

This section documents features not used directly in the Bayes Factor example but available in the text Datalog syntax.

10.1 Aggregation#

Aggregations summarise a set of values into a single result per group using the AGGREGATE construct:

ans(x, cnt) :- AGGREGATE[x](body(x, y) @ count(y)) = cnt.

The group-by variables appear in square brackets. The @ sign separates the body from the aggregation function call.

Supported functions: count, sum, max, min, mean, avg, std.

count(y) counts the distinct values of y in each group, and count(y, z) the distinct pairs: count(s) over schaefer_activated(l, s) counts each study once, however many parcels l it activates. The other functions take a value per row of the body: sum(w) over weight(s, w) adds the weights of all the studies s, equal or not, and mean(v) over the replicates rep(b, v) of a resampling averages all of them.

Example — count studies per region:

study_count(r, cnt) :-
    AGGREGATE[r](activates(s, r) @ count(s)) = cnt.

Global aggregation (no group-by) uses empty brackets:

total_studies(cnt) :-
    AGGREGATE[](study(s) @ count(s)) = cnt.

An aggregation can also be written directly in the head of a rule, the variables of the head being the group-by variables. This form accepts aggregation functions with constant parameters, such as percentile(v, q): the q-th percentile of v, with q in [0, 100], ignoring NaN values. It keeps, for instance, the top 10% of the scores of each query:

cutoff(q, percentile(v, 90)) :- score(q, s, v).
top_score(q, s) :- score(q, s, v), cutoff(q, t), (v >= t).

Percentiles are linearly interpolated and ties are kept, so the top 10% can hold slightly more than 10% of the rows.

Arithmetic in the head of a rule, ans(x, n / 2) :- r(x, n), is not an aggregation, and is refused: a value computed from each row is computed in the body, and named in the head, ans(x, v) :- r(x, n), (v == n / 2); a value computed from an aggregation, by a rule of its own over the aggregation’s rule.

10.2 Existential Predicates#

The exists construct asserts that there exists a tuple satisfying a set of conditions, without binding the existential variable in the rule head:

has_activations(r) :- region(r), exists(s; activates(s, r)).

This is equivalent to:

has_activations(r) :- region(r), activates(s, r).

The ; separates the existential variable from the body predicates. Multiple existential variables can be stacked:

ans(r) :- exists(s, t; activates(s, r), mentions(s, t)).

10.3 Comparisons#

Besides equality (==), the full comparison suite is available:

large_items(x) :- item(x), item_count(x, c), (c >= 2).
not_empty(x) :- item_count(x, c), (c != 0).
small(x) :- item(x), item_count(x, c), (c < 10).

Comparisons can be combined with conjunction in the same rule body.

10.4 Commands and Directives#

Commands use the . prefix and follow the Prolog convention. The text Datalog frontend executes two commands which load external data into a relation:

.load_csv(study_metadata, "https://example.com/metadata.tsv", sep="\t")
.load_atlas(destrieux, "atlas.nii.gz", "labels.csv", "https://example.com/destrieux.tgz")
.load_atlas(fs, "aparc+aseg.mgz", "FreeSurferColorLUT.txt", kind="freesurfer", space="MNI152")
.load_atlas(schaefer, kind="schaefer", n_rois=100)

.load_atlas adds the relation name(label, region) of an atlas: an image and a CSV of its labels at URLs (the first form, downloaded to ~/neurolang_data), local files of kind "freesurfer" (a colour LUT), "bids" (a dseg.tsv) or "tsv", the atlases of the engines ("destrieux", "schaefer", "tian") or a fetcher of nilearn (kind="nilearn", fetcher="harvard_oxford"). The engine records the atlas, a Atlas, by name, in its atlases.

and .import, which imports a Datalog module, a file of facts, rules and commands, as if its statements were written in its place:

.import("bundles/uncinate.dl")
.import("bundles/freesurfer_queries.dl")
ans(x) :- uf(x, "left").

  • The path of a module is relative to the directory of the importing file: the program run with neurolang-query --file, or the module importing another; otherwise it is relative to the working directory. A module not found there is looked for in the directories an engine gives its modules (module_directories of its solver): with the engine tracts, .import("bundles/freesurfer_queries.dl") is one of its modules. package:path is the file path shipped in the Python package package, found with importlib.resources, so that an engine or a package can ship modules without their users knowing where it is installed.

  • A module is imported once in a program (by its resolved path): importing it again, from the program or from another module, does nothing. The imports belong to the scope they are made in: leaving a with nl.scope: block, or .clear in the REPL, forgets them with the rules they defined, and the module can be imported again.

  • A module imported while it is being imported is an import cycle, an error naming the modules of the cycle.

  • A module defines; it does not query: a module with a query (ans(...) :- ...) is refused. An error in a module names its file, with the line and column of a syntax error.

NeuroLang in Python imports modules from Python.

Any other command (for instance .set_backend('polars')) is rejected with an UnsupportedProgramError. To change the relational algebra backend ('pandas' or 'polars') from Datalog code, use the SQUALL #set_backend directive, or Python (NeuroLang in Python); see Performance and Configuration. Note that the backend is a process-wide setting.

10.5 Probabilistic Facts#

Explicit probabilistic facts can be written inline:

0.9 :: noisy_region('fusiform').

This creates a probabilistic fact with probability 0.9.

Probabilistic rules (with a probability expression instead of a fixed probability) also exist:

p(x) :: exp(-d / 5.0) :- a(x, d), (d < 0.8).

10.6 Probabilistic Choices#

Probabilistic choices represent mutually exclusive alternatives — exactly one alternative from each choice group holds in any possible world. This is different from probabilistic facts (::), where each fact is an independent Bernoulli trial.

The choice operators are ^ and :~: (both are equivalent):

Choice fact — a standalone choice with a fixed probability:

0.3 ^ choice('a').
0.7 ^ choice('b').

This declares two mutually exclusive alternatives: choice('a') holds with probability 0.3 and choice('b') holds with probability 0.7. The probabilities in a choice group must sum to 1.

Choice rule — a choice whose head depends on a body:

n_studies(n) :- AGGREGATE[](study(s) @ count(s)) = n.
picked_study(s) ^ (1 / n) :- study(s), n_studies(n).

Here the probability expression (1 / n) is evaluated at runtime, with n bound in the body to the number of studies; each tuple in the body produces one arm of the choice, so picked_study is a uniform choice over studies. The :~: operator can be used interchangeably:

picked_study(s) :~: (1 / n) :- study(s), n_studies(n).

10.7 Confidence Intervals by Resampling#

The Bayes factors of Part 9 are computed from one sample of studies. How much they would change with another sample is answered by resampling the studies: a resampling of the choice selected_study is a set of replicates b, each reweighting the studies as a bootstrap of them does. It is declared once, in the engine’s configuration (Engine Configuration (engines.yaml)) or from Python (NeuroLang in Python): the neurosynth engine has boot_studies, 500 replicates of a Bayesian bootstrap of selected_study.

RESAMPLE[boot_studies](pred(x)) = b in a rule body is then pred(x) computed on replicate b, for any pred defined over PROB of the choice, including the arithmetic over the probabilities. The confidence interval is the percentiles of the replicates:

bf_rep(b, r, t, bf) :- RESAMPLE[boot_studies](bayes_factor(r, t, bf)) = b

ans(t, bf, percentile(x, 2.5), percentile(x, 97.5)) :-
    bayes_factor('R G oc temp lat fusifor', t, bf),
    bf_rep(b, 'R G oc temp lat fusifor', t, x),
    (bf > 4)

With the rules of Part 9, this gives the 95% intervals of the six terms of Bayes factor above 4, in under a minute: ffa, 8.92 [5.96, 13.64]; face ffa, 8.43 [5.40, 13.23]; fusiform gyri, 7.50 [4.90, 11.28]; fusiform face, 7.34 [5.39, 10.23]; visual stream, 4.50 [2.92, 7.09]; fusiform, 4.18 [3.73, 4.69].

The replicates of a resampling are the same in every query, so that two measures computed on them can be compared replicate by replicate, joining them on b:

difference(b, d) :-
    bf_rep(b, 'R G oc temp lat fusifor', 'ffa', x),
    bf_rep(b, 'R G oc temp lat fusifor', 'navigation', y),
    (d == log(x) - log(y))
ans(percentile(d, 2.5), percentile(d, 97.5)) :- difference(b, d)

The interval of the log ratio, [0.30, 1.51], excludes 0: ffa is more specific to the region than navigation (3.57), in this sample of studies.

The interval of a difference cannot be obtained from the intervals of the two measures, which are computed on the same studies and are correlated.

Schemes. scheme sets the weight of each study in a replicate: "bayesian", the Bayesian bootstrap (Rubin, 1981, Ann. Stat.), weights of a uniform Dirichlet distribution, never zero, so that a rare term keeps a defined Bayes factor; "multinomial", the classical bootstrap, how many times the study is drawn among N draws with replacement; "poisson", a Poisson(1) count. seed fixes the draws.

How it is computed. If the choice C takes the outcome c with probability \(P(C = c)\) and the replicate b with probability \(P_b(C = c) = w_{bc}\, P(C = c)\), then for any query q, as the choice holds for exactly one outcome in every world,

\[P_b(q) = \sum_c P_b(C = c)\, P(q \mid C = c) = \sum_c w_{bc}\, P(q \wedge C = c).\]

\(P(q \wedge C = c)\) is q with the outcome of the choice as one more answer variable, which the probabilistic solvers compute as any other query, and the sum over c is an aggregation joined with the resampling relation boot_studies(b, c, w). Hence RESAMPLE is a rewriting of the rules pred depends on and needs no other solver:

  1. the rules below a PROB that depend on the choice take the outcome c as their first argument, the same for all the atoms of a body, since all the atoms of the choice hold for the same outcome;

  2. each PROB over them becomes \(P(q \wedge C = c)\), then its sum weighted by each replicate; a conditional PROB[q // e] is the ratio of two of them;

  3. the rules over these probabilities take the replicate b as their first argument, the same for all the atoms of a body, so that the probabilities a measure combines come from the same replicate.

The rewritten rules are named after the resampling (bayes_factor@boot_studies) and are added to the program once, then reused by every RESAMPLE of the same predicate. Each replicate recomputes the measure, so that a query over n replicates costs about n times its rows: ask for one region or term rather than for every voxel.

Appendix B: Querying from the Command Line#

The neurolang-query command-line tool lets you run Datalog (or SQUALL) queries without writing Python code. It picks up a preconfigured engine from the engine registry and executes the query directly.

Listing available engines and predicates:

neurolang-query --list-engines
neurolang-query --engine neurosynth --list-predicates

Running a Datalog query inline:

neurolang-query --engine neurosynth \
    "ans(t) :- term_in_study_tfidf(s, t, w)"

Running a query from a file:

neurolang-query --engine neurosynth -f query.dl

Sorting the output by a column of the answer (--sort can be repeated; append :desc for descending order):

neurolang-query --engine neurosynth \
    --sort "w:desc" \
    "ans(t, w) :- term_in_study_tfidf(s, t, w)"

Saving and viewing the results#

--save PATH also writes the result in a file, and --view shows its brain image in the viewer of the interactive mode, as the REPL’s .save and .view do for its last result (see .help save and .help view there for the details). They need the tui extra (pip install "neurolang[tui]").

  • --save out.csv or out.tsv (optionally .gz) writes the table of the result, sorted as --sort asks.

  • --save out.nii or out.nii.gz writes a NIfTI image on the grid of the engine’s template. The voxels are the columns of the result of a sort the engine declares as voxel indices, or as millimetres, such as i, j, k; the value of a voxel is its probability or its one other numeric column, such as a count, else 1. A SQUALL program of several obtain statements gives a file per statement.

  • --view writes the viewer’s HTML page, prints its path and opens it in a browser when the standard error is a terminal.

For instance, the map of the number of studies mentioning pain that report each voxel:

neurolang-query --engine neurosynth --save pain.nii.gz --view \
    "n_pain(i, j, k, count(s)) :- reports(s, i, j, k), mentions(s, 'pain').
     ans(i, j, k, n) :- n_pain(i, j, k, n)"

A result on the regions of an atlas, a column of region labels such as l of destrieux_activated(l, s), is painted region by region, on the atlas’s grid, with the value of its rows; --view starts with the regions of the top 5% of the values, as for voxels (--threshold changes it), and with every region of a result without values. The probability that a study mentioning language activates each Destrieux region:

neurolang-query --engine neurosynth --view \
    "active(l, s) :- destrieux_activated(l, s), selected_study(s)
     term_association(s, t) :- mentions(s, t), selected_study(s)
     ans(l, p) :- PROB[active(l, s) // term_association(s, 'language')] = p"

The viewer names the region of the result under the mouse, and under the cursor below the views; its control atlas colours draws the regions in the colours their atlas gives them: Schaefer’s networks, FreeSurfer’s for Destrieux (aparc.a2009s), those of Tian’s subcortex. A result of regions alone, ans(l) :- schaefer_label(l), startswith('17Networks LH Default', l), starts in them.

A result of pairs of regions, or of points (x1, y1, z1, x2, y2, z2), with a value is a network: --view draws a node at the centre of each region and an edge per pair, its colour and thickness the value, from the thinnest at the edge threshold to the thickest at the largest value, in the 3D render of the brain and on the slices, and lists the strongest edges. The edge threshold, the strongest 5% by default (--threshold), is changed in the viewer, under the views: “Edges from” and its slider draw the network again with the edges above it. On a slice, the mouse over a node shows the name of its region, and over an edge the names of the two regions it joins and its value. The co-activation of the Schaefer regions by studies about reading, the probability that a study mentioning reading activates both regions of a pair:

neurolang-query --engine neurosynth --view \
    "active(l, s) :- schaefer_activated(l, s), selected_study(s)
     co_active(l1, l2, s) :- active(l1, s), active(l2, s)
     term_association(s, t) :- mentions(s, t), selected_study(s)
     ans(l1, l2, p) :- (l1 < l2), PROB[co_active(l1, l2, s) // term_association(s, 'reading')] = p"

Both work with --ask, which then runs the query it writes.

Where an answer comes from#

In the interactive mode, .graph draws the last query, run or written by the assistant with .ask, in a page opened in the browser; the REPL in the browser shows it in its Graph tab after each query. It reads from left to right: the data of the engine (its relations, its random choices, such as selected_study, and its probabilistic facts), the rules of the query and of the engine they go through, and the answer. A rule lists the atoms of its body, where the edges of the relations they use arrive, and how it combines them: the variables it joins them on, and those it keeps or drops. A rule using a random choice is probabilistic: its rows hold for some choices only (orange edges). PROB[...] turns it into the probability of each of its rows, given the condition after // (dashed edges): with one uniform choice, such as selected_study, the share of the studies where the body and the condition hold among those where the condition holds. After a run, the answer shows the distribution of its probabilities. Clicking a node shows its rules and what it depends on. The relations that come from an ontology, such as the triples of the Cognitive Atlas (rdfs:label), are teal hexagons, apart from the blue cylinders of the regular tables.

Inspecting query plans#

--show-ra (-Q) prints, instead of the answer, the relational algebra plan each rule of the query is evaluated with, stratum by stratum (deterministic, probabilistic and post-probabilistic). Plans are drawn as trees; base relations are shown as atoms with their number of rows, the predicate generated for the query is shown as ans, probabilistic plans name the solver that produced them (small dichotomy theorem, Dalvi–Suciu lifted inference or weighted model counting) and label their probability columns p, and recursive rules are shown once, followed by the number of iterations the fixpoint took. The rewritten program is printed too when --show-rewritten is given. Two flags refine the display:

  • --ra-verbose prints the plans in full, without collapsing the renames and projections of base relations into atoms (ρ[x, y](π[#0, #1](edge)) instead of edge(x, y));

  • --ra-analyze runs the query and annotates every plan node with the number of rows it produced, like SQL’s EXPLAIN ANALYZE. Counts that would require removing duplicates of values that are expensive to compare, such as regions, are upper bounds, printed ‹≤N rows›.

For instance:

$ neurolang-query --engine neurosynth --show-ra --ra-analyze \
    "ans(l, s) :- reports(s, i, j, k), schaefer_voxels(i, j, k, l)."
── deterministic stratum ──
── rule reports(s, i, j, k) :-
    peak_reported(i, j, k, s) ──
peak_reported(i, j, k, s) ‹447,477 rows›
── rule schaefer_voxels(I, J, K, l) :-
    schaefer(l, r),
    ⟨contains⟩(r, (I, J, K)) ──
δ[r→('I', 'J', 'K')] ‹≤132,032 rows›
└─ schaefer(l, r) ‹100 rows›
── rule ans(l, s) :-
    reports(s, i, j, k),
    schaefer_voxels(i, j, k, l) ──
⋈ ‹10,190 rows›
├─ reports(s, i, j, k) ‹447,477 rows›
└─ schaefer_voxels(i, j, k, l) ‹132,032 rows›

From Python, pass show_ra=True to execute_datalog_program, execute_squall_program or query, or a ShowRAOptions such as ShowRAOptions(collapse=False, analyze=True).

For the full list of options, see neurolang-query --help or the neurolang.utils.cli module documentation.
